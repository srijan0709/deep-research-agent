# Research Report

## Executive Summary

A hypothetical U.S. tariff of 100% on Indian goods would most likely worsen India’s current account deficit (CAD) by reducing India’s merchandise exports to the United States. The tariff would be imposed by the United States on imports entering its market, rather than directly on Indian firms or on India’s current account.[5][18][23]

The size of the effect cannot be reliably quantified from the evidence provided. It depends on several missing variables: the value of India’s exports to the United States, the share of those exports covered by the tariff, the extent to which U.S. demand falls, how much of the tariff is absorbed by Indian exporters or U.S. importers, and whether trade is redirected to other markets.

The likely first-round effect is a deterioration in India’s goods trade balance and therefore its CAD. However, the final effect could be smaller if India reduces imports, increases exports to other countries, gains from currency depreciation, or offsets lost goods exports through services receipts and remittances. A 100% tariff would therefore represent a significant downside risk to India’s external balance, but the available sources do not support a defensible numerical estimate.

## Key Findings

1. **The tariff would be a U.S. import tax.** It would be collected when Indian goods enter the United States, consistent with the general operation of tariffs.[5][18][23]

2. **The direct channel would be lower Indian goods exports to the United States.** A 100% tariff would sharply increase the U.S. landed price of affected Indian products unless exporters, importers, or intermediaries absorb some of the cost.

3. **Lower goods exports would tend to worsen India’s CAD.** In simplified terms, India’s current account depends on its goods trade balance, services balance, primary-income balance, and transfers. A fall in merchandise exports, with other components unchanged, reduces the current-account balance and increases the deficit.

4. **The tariff would not automatically reduce India’s total exports by 100%.** Indian producers could redirect goods to other markets, alter product composition, accept lower margins, or shift production and supply chains. The actual export loss would depend on demand elasticity and the scope of the tariff.

5. **Import compression could partially offset the damage.** Weaker exports and slower domestic activity could reduce India’s demand for energy, capital goods, intermediate inputs, and consumer imports. That would improve the trade balance relative to a scenario in which imports did not change.

6. **Services and remittances could cushion, but not necessarily eliminate, the effect.** India’s services exports and transfers from abroad could continue to support the current account. The supplied evidence, however, does not establish their size or likely response to this specific tariff shock.

7. **The net effect is uncertain and cannot be calculated from the supplied evidence.** No verified figures were provided for India’s U.S.-bound exports, the tariff-covered product base, import elasticities, or India’s baseline CAD.

## Detailed Analysis

### 1. How the tariff would operate

A 100% tariff is most naturally interpreted as an ad valorem import duty equal to 100% of the relevant customs value. Tariffs are imposed by the importing country on goods entering its market and are generally collected at the border or point of importation.[5][18][23]

For example, if an Indian product has a customs value of 100 before the tariff, a 100% duty would create a tariff liability of 100, before accounting for other charges. The resulting U.S. landed cost would rise substantially. The precise consumer-facing price increase could differ because firms may absorb part of the tariff, renegotiate contracts, reduce margins, or change sourcing.

The tariff would therefore be a tax on access to the U.S. market. It would not directly debit India’s current-account balance. Its effect on the CAD would arise indirectly through changes in exports, imports, income flows, and economic activity.

### 2. Goods-export channel

Let India’s current-account balance be represented schematically as:

\[
CA = (X_g - M_g) + (X_s - M_s) + NI + TR
\]

where:

- \(X_g\) and \(M_g\) are goods exports and imports;
- \(X_s\) and \(M_s\) are services exports and imports;
- \(NI\) is net primary income;
- \(TR\) is net transfers, including remittances.

If the tariff reduces India’s exports to the United States, \(X_g\) falls. Holding all other components constant:

\[
\Delta CA \approx \Delta X_g < 0
\]

The CAD would therefore widen by approximately the value of the lost export receipts, expressed in foreign currency, if there were no offsetting changes.

The actual decline in exports would likely be less than the full value of shipments exposed to the tariff if:

- U.S. importers absorb some of the cost;
- Indian exporters reduce their prices;
- firms redirect shipments to other countries;
- buyers switch only partially to alternative suppliers;
- products receive exemptions or preferential treatment; or
- the tariff is avoided through changes in production location or supply chains.

Conversely, the decline could be severe for products with close substitutes and highly price-sensitive demand.

### 3. Trade diversion and export reorientation

India might redirect some affected exports from the United States to Europe, Asia, the Middle East, or other markets. This would limit the reduction in India’s total exports, although it might require lower prices or additional logistics costs.

Trade diversion would not necessarily fully neutralize the shock. Other markets may not have enough demand to absorb the displaced goods, and selling at lower prices could reduce export earnings even if physical export volumes remain relatively stable.

The composition of India’s exports would also matter. Products that are differentiated, specialized, or difficult to replace may experience a smaller volume decline than standardized goods. The available evidence does not identify the product categories covered by the hypothetical tariff, so this channel cannot be quantified.

### 4. Import-compression channel

A tariff-induced export shock could weaken production, investment, employment, and income in affected sectors. Lower domestic activity may reduce imports, particularly:

- fuel and other commodities;
- intermediate inputs used in export production;
- machinery and capital goods;
- consumer goods.

Lower imports improve the goods trade balance and therefore offset part of the initial deterioration. However, this adjustment would come partly through weaker economic activity, so it would not represent an unambiguously positive outcome.

There could also be an input-cost effect. If Indian exporters use imported components, falling export production could reduce imports of those components. This would cushion the CAD impact, but it could also reduce the competitiveness and output of affected industries.

### 5. Exchange-rate channel

A deterioration in export receipts could place downward pressure on the Indian rupee, depending on capital flows and foreign-exchange-market conditions. A weaker rupee could make Indian goods cheaper in foreign-currency terms and make imports more expensive in rupee terms.

This could support export adjustment and suppress imports over time. But the effect is ambiguous:

- imported fuel and raw materials would become more expensive;
- foreign-currency debt servicing could become costlier;
- inflation could increase;
- the U.S. tariff might prevent the exchange-rate adjustment from restoring competitiveness in the U.S. market.

Thus, depreciation could moderate the CAD deterioration without necessarily eliminating it.

### 6. Services, investment income, and remittances

India’s current account is broader than its merchandise trade balance. Strong net services receipts, remittances, or other transfer inflows could offset part of the lost goods-export earnings.

The tariff could also affect services indirectly. For example, weaker goods trade could reduce transport, logistics, and trade-related services. On the other hand, firms seeking to avoid tariffs might increase cross-border business, technology, or professional-services activity, depending on the policy design.

Primary-income flows could move in either direction. A weaker economy might reduce returns earned by Indian residents abroad, while changes in foreign investment income could affect payments to non-residents. The supplied evidence contains no data with which to estimate these effects.

### 7. Possible macroeconomic scenarios

Because the necessary baseline data are unavailable, the most defensible approach is scenario analysis rather than a point estimate.

**Mild-impact scenario**

- The tariff applies to a limited set of products.
- U.S. importers and Indian exporters share much of the burden.
- India redirects a large share of shipments to other markets.
- Services receipts and remittances remain resilient.
- Imports decline as export-related activity weakens.

Under this scenario, India’s CAD worsens, but only modestly.

**Intermediate-impact scenario**

- The tariff covers a broad range of Indian goods.
- Demand for affected products falls substantially in the United States.
- Some exports are diverted, but at lower prices.
- Import compression offsets part of the lost exports.
- Services and remittances provide partial support.

Under this scenario, the CAD deteriorates noticeably, with a broader negative effect on growth and the rupee.

**Severe-impact scenario**

- The tariff is broad and strictly enforced.
- Indian goods become uncompetitive in the U.S. market.
- Trade diversion is limited.
- Retaliatory measures or policy uncertainty disrupt wider trade.
- Services, investment, and remittance flows also weaken.

Under this scenario, India’s goods trade deficit and CAD could widen materially. The impact would be amplified if energy prices remained high or capital inflows weakened simultaneously.

## Conflicting Evidence

No directly conflicting estimates were supplied. The available material consists mainly of general tariff definitions and general country-information sources, rather than empirical estimates of India’s CAD sensitivity to a U.S. tariff.

There is, however, an important analytical distinction between two possible interpretations:

- A **mechanical interpretation** assumes that the value of exports lost to the U.S. directly widens the CAD by the same amount.
- A **general-equilibrium interpretation** allows for import compression, trade diversion, exchange-rate adjustment, services receipts, remittances, and changes in investment or income flows.

The mechanical estimate would generally overstate the final CAD effect because it ignores these offsets. But without data on the relevant elasticities and flows, the general-equilibrium effect cannot be calculated reliably.

## Areas of Uncertainty

The following information is necessary for a quantitative estimate but is not present in the supplied evidence:

1. India’s total goods exports to the United States.
2. The share of those exports subject to the hypothetical tariff.
3. The customs valuation and whether the tariff would be 100% ad valorem across all products.
4. Demand elasticities for affected Indian exports.
5. The extent to which U.S. importers or Indian exporters would absorb the tariff.
6. India’s ability to redirect exports to third-country markets.
7. Changes in India’s imports following an export shock.
8. The response of services exports, remittances, and primary-income flows.
9. Exchange-rate and capital-flow responses.
10. Possible exemptions, retaliation, transshipment, or changes in supply-chain location.

The cited sources establish the general nature and operation of tariffs, but they do not provide the India-specific macroeconomic data needed to produce a credible percentage-point or dollar estimate for the CAD.[5][18][23]

## Conclusion

A U.S. 100% tariff on Indian goods would probably worsen India’s current account deficit through a reduction in merchandise export earnings. The direct effect would be strongest for products that are price-sensitive and easily replaceable in the U.S. market.

The final impact would be smaller than the initial export loss if India reduced imports, redirected exports, experienced currency adjustment, or maintained strong services and remittance inflows. It could be larger if the tariff triggered broader trade disruption, weakened investment, or affected services and capital flows.

Accordingly, the best-supported conclusion is directional rather than numerical: **the tariff would create significant downward pressure on India’s current-account balance, but the available evidence is insufficient to quantify how much the CAD would widen.**
